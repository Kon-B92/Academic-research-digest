## AI & Learning Environments

### Classroom Social Climate, AI Literacy, and Student Engagement in AI-Mediated English Learning: Linear and Configurational Perspectives
- **Authors:** Not confirmed from available sources (see transparency note)
- **Source:** *Behavioral Sciences* (MDPI), 2026, 16(11), 2137
- **Publication date:** 10 November 2026
- **URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/11/2137
- **Keywords matched:** learning environments (classroom social climate), AI in education, student engagement, SDT

Surveying 570 Chinese university EFL students who use AI tools (SEM + fsQCA), the study finds classroom social climate and AI literacy each relate to engagement both directly and indirectly through resilience and self-efficacy, with several distinct "configurations" leading to high engagement. For teaching quality, the message is that AI tools do not replace the human classroom climate: instructors who build a supportive social environment *and* teach AI literacy get more engaged learners than either lever alone. Through an SDT lens, climate maps onto relatedness and AI literacy/self-efficacy onto competence, suggesting AI-mediated courses still depend on meeting basic psychological needs.

### Surrendering and Reclaiming Judgment: How Software Engineering Students Experience AI Reliance
- **Authors:** Not confirmed from available sources
- **Source:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2611.12646)
- **Publication date:** November 2026 (per arXiv ID-month convention)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12646
- **Keywords matched:** AI in education, student agency, SDT (autonomy/competence/relatedness)

In a qualitative study of software engineering students working on AI-assisted projects, students described handing over not just effort but *judgment* to AI, and this went along with lost motivation. Some regained it when deadlines were relaxed and when a dedicated lecture and lab on working with AI was added. For instructional effectiveness, this points to course design choices (pacing, explicit teaching of AI oversight strategies) as practical ways to protect learning. The authors frame the loss of ownership as a weakening of autonomy and competence, and suggest that using AI instead of peers may also weaken relatedness.

### "Can a Robot Really Care About Me?" How Perceiving a Tutor as Human or AI Affects Computer Science Students' Attitudes and Experiences
- **Authors:** Not confirmed from available sources
- **Source:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2611.20367)
- **Publication date:** November 2026 (per arXiv ID-month convention) **[likely 7-day: high 2611 sequence number]**
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20367
- **Keywords matched:** AI in education, SDT, student engagement (enjoyment)

Introductory CS students had a tutoring session with the same tutor, labelled either "human" or "AI". Ratings of autonomy support, relatedness and enjoyment leaned toward the human label while competence was about equal, but no difference survived correction for multiple comparisons (a small sample of brief first sessions; one search result links it to a Cal Poly thesis with 27 participants). For teaching quality, this suggests that *being seen as* human may matter most for the relational side of tutoring, not for skill gains. In SDT terms, AI tutors may meet the competence need about as well as humans, while relatedness is the need most at risk.

## DEI & Belonging

### Understanding Computing Instructors' Perceptions of Student Sense of Belonging
- **Authors:** Not confirmed from available sources
- **Source:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2611.13237)
- **Publication date:** November 2026 (per arXiv ID-month convention)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13237
- **Keywords matched:** sense of belonging, learning environments (course climate), student engagement/inclusion at scale

Nine semi-structured interviews with introductory computing instructors at four large US R1 public universities explore how instructors understand and assess belonging, how they try to support it, and what stops them from doing so in large courses. It adds the teaching side to a field built mostly on student surveys, and points to using teaching assistants to build belonging at scale. For student needs, the work treats relatedness as something course structure and staffing can design for, not left to individual instructors' goodwill. It also notes how students' expectations of computing and wider social trends affect who feels they belong.
