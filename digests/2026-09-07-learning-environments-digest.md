## AI, Epistemic Authority & Student Agency (SDT)

### Reclaiming Epistemic Agency: A Critical Framework for Human-Generative AI Co-Agency in Education
- **Authors:** Biranchi Poudyal
- **Source:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2608.26937), category cs.CY (Computers and Society)
- **Publication date:** ~26 August 2026 (per arXiv ID convention and search metadata)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26937

This conceptual paper argues that generative AI is quietly reassigning *epistemic* authority in classrooms — the power to produce knowledge claims, validate them, and count as evidence of learning — shifting it from teacher to student to machine, and analyzes this shift through Distributed Agency and Self-Determination Theory alongside TPACK/SAMR technology-integration paradigms. For teaching quality, the framework is a call for instructors to deliberately design *who* gets to validate a claim in an AI-assisted classroom, rather than letting default tool behavior silently absorb that role. Through the SDT lens, unexamined delegation of validation to AI risks eroding student autonomy (ownership of one's own reasoning) and competence (a sense of earned, evaluated mastery) even where it superficially expands access to help.

## Comparative Learning Environments Research & Student Well-Being

### What are the key elements of a positive learning environment? Perspectives from students and faculty
- **Authors:** Gail Elliott, Justin Kowal, Grace Pinhal-Enfield
- **Source:** *Learning Environments Research* (Springer), Article 47/48 online-first
- **Publication date:** 28 August 2026
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/journal/10984 (a stable direct article permalink could not be resolved via search — see transparency note)

Drawing on open-ended comments from 88 students in a redesigned graduate-level gross anatomy course (Master of Biomedical Sciences, Rutgers University), this study identifies psychological safety and faculty engagement as the elements students and faculty most associate with a positive learning environment after a curriculum redesign. For instructional effectiveness, it points to faculty responsiveness — not just content or format changes — as the practical lever a redesign should protect. Read through Self-Determination Theory, psychological safety functions as an entry point to relatedness (a felt faculty–student connection) and competence (safety to make and correct mistakes while learning), both preconditions for genuine engagement in high-stakes course material.
